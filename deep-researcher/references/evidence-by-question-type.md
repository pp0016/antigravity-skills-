# What a good source looks like, by question type

Read only the section that matches the question. In each, sources are ordered
from strongest to weakest, followed by the check that catches the most common
failure for that type. The orderings for health and software rest on measured
findings; those for business, news and creator platforms are standard expert
practice. The rule underneath all of them: get as close as you can to whoever
produced the fact, then check the thing that usually goes wrong in that field.

## Health, medicine, science

1. Current clinical guideline, systematic review or meta-analysis (Cochrane,
   national health bodies)
2. Large randomised or pre-registered trial
3. Observational study
4. Preprint — usable, labelled as a preprint; many are never published
5. Press release about a study
6. News coverage of the press release

Checks: search the paper title with "retraction" (retracted papers keep being
cited for years). Check that the journal is a recognised one; "peer-reviewed"
on the journal's own site proves nothing. Read the abstract or full text, not
the news summary. One new study does not overturn a review of many. Note
sample size, who was studied, and who funded it.

## Statistics and "X% of..." claims

1. The producing body's own dataset or release, with its methodology note
2. A report that reproduces the table and links to it
3. A news article quoting it
4. Infographics, listicles, social posts

Checks: what was measured, out of what, on what sample, when; headline figure
or subgroup; latest release or an old one. "50% more than" and "50% of" are
different claims and get swapped constantly. If the origin cannot be found,
the number is a lead, not a finding, and he should not quote it.

## Software, tools, AI models, APIs

1. Source code, release notes, changelog, official docs for the version in use
2. Issue tracker and maintainer comments
3. Standards and specifications
4. Engineering blogs from teams that run the thing in production
5. Q&A sites — check the answer's date and version
6. Tutorials and content-marketing blogs

Checks: version and date. Being out of date, not dishonesty, is the main
failure here; many answers are obsolete when posted. For anything released in
the last few weeks, official sources only, since everything else predates it.
Benchmarks published by a vendor about its own product are claims until an
independent party reproduces them.

## Market size, business, money

1. Audited filings (annual reports, 10-K, prospectus), regulators, national
   statistics
2. Earnings calls and investor decks — primary for what the company claims,
   not for whether it is true
3. Named analyst reports with a disclosed method
4. Press-release summaries of paid market reports — leads only
5. Blogs quoting those

Checks: treat any "USD X billion by 20YY at Z% CAGR" sentence as marketing
until you can see the method. Different firms define the same market
differently, so report a range with the definitions, never a single figure.
Income claims from people selling a course are advertisements.

## News and current events

1. The outlet that did the original reporting; wire services (AP, Reuters,
   AFP); primary documents (court filings, official statements, transcripts)
2. Outlets that credit and link the above
3. Aggregators and rewrites
4. Social posts

Checks: follow "first reported by" credits and publication times to find the
originating outlet. For breaking stories, early reports are often wrong; date
everything and prefer later corrected accounts.

## YouTube, creator platforms, algorithm and monetisation claims

1. The platform's own documentation, policy pages, official blog and official
   staff channels (for YouTube: Help Center, Creator Insider, official
   statements from named staff)
2. Studies over many channels or videos with a stated sample and method
3. A named creator's own analytics, shown on screen, for their own channel —
   evidence about that channel only
4. "What the algorithm wants" videos and blogs, tool vendors' growth claims,
   anonymous forum advice — leads only

Checks: policy and monetisation rules change often and differ by country, so
confirm on the current policy page and note the date you read it. A tactic
that worked for one channel is an anecdote; look for whether it holds across
many, and whether the person recommending it sells something that depends on
it. RPM, CPM and revenue figures vary widely by niche, audience country and
season, so report ranges with their source, not single numbers.
